Why Is My Commercial Baler Not Working? 10 Common Problems and When to Call for Service

2026-09-21 13 min read Norton Equipment

Published by Norton Equipment Co., serving commercial waste and recycling equipment customers from Byhalia, Mississippi since 1997.

The short answer

If your commercial baler will not start, cycle, tie, or eject, record the symptom and any fault code. Check only normal operator controls under the machine manual and site procedures. Never bypass safeguards or attempt internal repairs. Contact a qualified baler technician when normal checks do not resolve the problem.

When a commercial baler stops working, the cardboard does not stop arriving. Within a short period of time, a clean receiving area can turn into stacks of boxes, blocked workspace, extra labor, dock congestion, and a growing operational problem. The first instinct may be to keep pressing buttons until something happens. That is not a good troubleshooting strategy. A commercial baler combines powerful hydraulic, electrical, and mechanical systems. The safest approach begins with operator-level observations and the manufacturer's normal operating procedures. Internal electrical, hydraulic, structural, or mechanical diagnosis should be performed by trained and authorized personnel. Below are ten common commercial baler problems, what the symptoms may indicate, and when it is time to call for service.

Before Troubleshooting Anything

Follow your facility's approved procedures and the operating instructions for your specific machine. Never:

  • Reach into the bale chamber
  • Climb into the machine
  • Bypass an interlock
  • Defeat a guard
  • Open electrical equipment unless qualified and authorized
  • Search for hydraulic leaks using your hand
  • Work beneath unsupported equipment components
  • Attempt internal repairs while hazardous energy remains present

If service work requires access to a hazardous area, approved lockout/tagout procedures should be followed by properly trained personnel. Production problems are frustrating. They are not worth creating an injury.

Problem #1: The Baler Will Not Start

You press the normal operating control and nothing happens. Several categories of problems can create this symptom. Possible causes may include:

  • Emergency stop condition
  • Open loading door
  • Open safety gate
  • Safety interlock issue
  • Facility power problem
  • Disconnect condition
  • Control problem
  • Motor starter problem
  • Electrical fault

What an operator can safely check

Using only the machine's normal operating controls:

  • Is an emergency stop activated?
  • Are required doors completely closed?
  • Are required gates completely closed?
  • Is there a visible warning or fault message?
  • Is there an obvious facility power problem?
  • Has the machine been intentionally disconnected or locked out?

Do not bypass a gate switch, door switch, interlock, or other safety device. If normal operating conditions appear correct and the baler still will not start, contact qualified service personnel.

Problem #2: The Baler Starts but Will Not Complete the Cycle

The baler begins operating but stops before completing its normal cycle. Potential categories include:

  • Sensor problem
  • Limit switch issue
  • Safety circuit interruption
  • Hydraulic problem
  • Control fault
  • Mechanical obstruction
  • Motor issue
  • Electrical issue

The most useful thing you can do is observe the pattern from a safe location. Ask:

  • Does it stop at the same point every cycle?
  • Does the ram move partway and stop?
  • Does the machine return normally?
  • Does a fault code appear?
  • Did the problem begin suddenly?
  • Does it happen only under load?

Document what happens. Instead of telling the technician: "The baler is broken." you can say: "The ram moves about halfway through the downstroke, stops at the same point, and displays this fault code." That can dramatically improve the starting point for diagnosis.

Problem #3: The Ram Is Moving Slowly

A noticeable change in cycle speed deserves attention. Potential causes can include:

  • Hydraulic fluid issues
  • Restricted filtration
  • Pump wear
  • Valve problems
  • Cylinder problems
  • Excessive operating temperature
  • Motor problems
  • Electrical conditions
  • Mechanical resistance

Do not begin adjusting hydraulic pressure because of a recommendation found online. Hydraulic settings are machine-specific. Incorrect adjustment can damage equipment and create safety risks. A qualified technician should diagnose why performance changed before settings are modified.

Problem #4: Hydraulic Fluid Is Leaking

Hydraulic leaks are among the most visible equipment problems. Leak sources may include:

  • Hoses
  • Fittings
  • Cylinders
  • Seals
  • Valves
  • Pumps
  • Connections
  • Other hydraulic components

A small leak should not automatically be treated as a small problem. Leaks can worsen. They can reduce machine performance. They can contaminate the work area. They can also create slip hazards.

Important hydraulic safety rule

Never use your hand or fingers to search for a pressurized hydraulic leak. If you observe leaking hydraulic fluid, follow approved shutdown procedures and contact trained service personnel.

Problem #5: The Baler Makes a New Banging, Grinding, Screeching, or Knocking Sound

Employees who operate the same baler every day may know the sound of the machine better than anyone else. Use that knowledge. A new sound can indicate:

  • Loose components
  • Mechanical wear
  • Alignment problems
  • Bearing problems
  • Hydraulic problems
  • Metal-to-metal contact
  • Damaged guides
  • Structural problems
  • Foreign material
  • A developing failure

Not every new sound means the machine is about to fail. But new sounds should be investigated. If the noise is severe, suddenly developed, or continues getting worse, stop operating the machine according to facility procedures and have it inspected.

Problem #6: The Bale Is Suddenly Loose or Misshapen

Finished bale quality is a useful diagnostic clue. If the baler previously produced consistent bales and that changes, possible causes may involve:

  • Material type
  • Loading pattern
  • Uneven material distribution
  • Hydraulic performance
  • Ram wear
  • Chamber wear
  • Tying problems
  • Incorrect wire
  • Equipment adjustment

Start by asking whether the process changed. Did the facility begin baling a different material? Did employees change loading procedures? Did you switch baling wire? Is material wetter or more contaminated than normal? If the material and operating process have not changed, the equipment may need inspection.

Problem #7: Baling Wire Keeps Breaking

Wire problems can stop production even when the baler itself continues compressing material. Possible factors include:

  • Incorrect wire specification
  • Improper tying
  • Damaged wire guides
  • Excessive bale expansion
  • Tying-system wear
  • Burrs
  • Sharp damaged surfaces
  • Changes in bale density
  • Poor wire storage
  • Incorrect wire routing

Make sure the facility is using baling wire appropriate for the machine and application. If the machine worked normally for months and suddenly begins breaking wire repeatedly, investigate the system instead of assuming every bundle of wire is defective.

Problem #8: A Horizontal Baler Will Not Tie Correctly

Automatic tying systems add productivity, but they also introduce additional components. Potential problem areas may include:

  • Wire feed
  • Tier components
  • Cutters
  • Sensors
  • Guides
  • Controls
  • Alignment
  • Mechanical wear

Tie failures can become especially disruptive in high-volume facilities because material may continue arriving while finished bales cannot be completed properly. Do not allow repeated tie problems to encourage employees to enter hazardous areas or improvise unsafe workarounds. Call for professional service.

Problem #9: The Bale Will Not Eject Normally

A bale that will not eject can create immediate frustration. Possible factors can include:

  • Improper bale formation
  • Ejection-system problems
  • Mechanical wear
  • Hydraulic issues
  • Damaged components
  • Material caught where it should not be
  • Incorrect operating procedure
  • Tying problems

Never place a person inside a hazardous area to push, pull, kick, or manually free a stuck bale during normal operation. If access is required for service, the equipment must be placed into the proper controlled condition according to manufacturer instructions and approved safety procedures. A stuck bale may cost time. An injury costs far more.

Problem #10: The Baler Keeps Tripping, Faulting, or Needing to Be Reset

This is one of the easiest bad habits to develop around older equipment: "Just reset it. It always does that." Repeated faults are not normal simply because they happen frequently. They are information. Potential causes may involve:

  • Electrical problems
  • Motor condition
  • Overheating
  • Control problems
  • Sensor issues
  • Safety circuits
  • Hydraulic loading
  • Mechanical resistance
  • Loose connections

When permitted by your normal operating procedure, record the fault information before resetting the machine. Useful information can include:

  • Fault code
  • Time of day
  • Material being processed
  • Stage of the cycle
  • Frequency
  • Whether the machine was hot
  • Whether the same condition occurs every time

If the machine continues to fault, have the underlying cause diagnosed.

Bonus Problem: The Baler Works Sometimes and Fails Other Times

Intermittent problems can be among the most frustrating failures to diagnose. The machine may work perfectly when a technician arrives. Then fail again the next day. Intermittent conditions may involve:

  • Sensors
  • Loose electrical connections
  • Heat
  • Wiring
  • Switches
  • Control components
  • Hydraulic conditions
  • Mechanical alignment
  • Vibration

Documentation becomes especially valuable here. Take note of:

  • Exactly when the failure occurs
  • Weather or temperature if equipment is outside
  • Whether the machine is hot or cold
  • How long it had been operating
  • Which employee was using it
  • Which material was being processed
  • What happened immediately before the failure

Patterns can reveal problems that initially seem random.

Do Not Keep Resetting a Machine Without Understanding Why

A reset can sometimes be part of a normal manufacturer-approved operating procedure. But repeated resetting should not replace diagnosis. Imagine a machine that shuts down three times every morning. Employees reset it each time. Eventually the failure becomes severe enough that the machine will not restart. The facility may have lost weeks or months of warning signs. Repeated faults deserve attention.

What Information Should You Give the Service Technician?

You can help speed up diagnosis by gathering useful information before calling. Have available:

  • Manufacturer
  • Model
  • Serial number
  • Machine location
  • Material being processed
  • Exact symptom
  • When the problem began
  • Whether it happens every cycle
  • Visible fault code
  • Photos from a safe location
  • Video from a safe location if useful
  • Recent repairs
  • Recent changes in material
  • Recent changes in baling wire
  • Visible leaks
  • Unusual noise
  • Whether the machine is currently operational

Specific information helps. Instead of: "Our baler won't work." say: "Our vertical baler powers on normally, but the ram stops about halfway through the downstroke. The issue started this morning and now happens every cycle. The display shows fault code XYZ." That gives a technician much more to work with.

When Should You Stop Using the Baler Immediately?

Certain conditions deserve immediate attention. Follow your company's shutdown procedures if you observe conditions such as:

  • Required safety-device failure
  • Significant hydraulic leak
  • Smoke
  • Electrical burning smell
  • Severe abnormal noise
  • Unexpected machine movement
  • Visible structural damage
  • Exposed electrical damage
  • Repeated uncontrolled faults
  • A condition that makes employees question whether the machine is safe

When safety is in question, production comes second.

Why Troubleshooting Gets Harder When Maintenance Records Do Not Exist

Suppose a technician arrives and asks: "Has the cycle time been slowing down?" Nobody knows. "When was the hydraulic system last serviced?" Nobody knows. "How long has that wire problem been happening?" Nobody knows. "Has this fault code appeared before?" Nobody knows. That makes diagnosis harder. A basic maintenance and fault log can provide valuable history. Track:

  • Fault codes
  • Service dates
  • Repairs
  • Parts replaced
  • Hydraulic work
  • Electrical work
  • Tie problems
  • Cycle changes
  • Operator observations

The more important the baler is to production, the more useful this information becomes.

Preventive Maintenance Can Catch Problems Before They Become Breakdowns

The best time to discover a deteriorating component is during scheduled maintenance. Not during the busiest shift of the week. A preventive maintenance program can help provide:

  • Service history
  • Wear tracking
  • Hydraulic inspection
  • Electrical inspection
  • Safety-system inspection
  • Planned parts replacement
  • Better documentation
  • Earlier identification of developing problems

It cannot eliminate every breakdown. It can reduce the number of surprises.

Repair or Replace?

Not every malfunction means the baler needs to be replaced. A qualified technician can help determine whether the problem involves:

  • A normal wear item
  • Hydraulic repair
  • Electrical repair
  • Control replacement
  • Cylinder repair
  • Structural damage
  • Tying-system repair
  • Several major systems failing together

Repair may make sense when the machine is fundamentally sound and correctly sized for the operation. Replacement may deserve consideration when:

  • Major failures are becoming frequent
  • Parts are difficult to obtain
  • The machine is badly undersized
  • Downtime is becoming expensive
  • Structural condition is poor
  • The business has outgrown the equipment
  • Repair costs continue increasing

The right answer should be based on the equipment and the economics of the operation.

Straight Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Why won't my cardboard baler start?
Possible causes range from normal operator conditions, such as an activated emergency stop or improperly closed gate, to safety circuit, control, motor, power, or electrical problems. Never bypass safeguards. If normal operating conditions are correct, contact qualified service personnel.
Why is my baler ram moving slowly?
Slow movement can involve hydraulic, electrical, mechanical, or load-related problems. A qualified technician should diagnose the cause rather than changing hydraulic settings without proper testing.
Is a small hydraulic leak safe to ignore?
No. Hydraulic leaks can worsen, affect machine performance, and create safety issues. Never use your hand to locate a pressurized hydraulic leak.
Can I bypass a baler door switch if it stops working?
No. Do not bypass required safety interlocks. Remove the equipment from service according to facility procedures and have the problem repaired properly.
Why does my baler keep tripping?
Repeated trips or faults can involve motors, controls, sensors, electrical problems, heat, safety circuits, hydraulic load, or mechanical resistance. Record the fault information and have recurring problems professionally diagnosed.
Why does my baling wire keep breaking?
Possible causes include incorrect wire, tying problems, wire guides, damaged surfaces, excessive bale expansion, changes in material, or tying-system wear. If wire failures begin suddenly, inspect the complete system.
Should I repair or replace an old baler?
It depends on the machine's structural condition, repair cost, parts availability, downtime, capacity, and how well the baler fits your current operation. An equipment evaluation can help determine which option provides better long-term value.
