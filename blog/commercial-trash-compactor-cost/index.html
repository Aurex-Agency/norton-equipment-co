Compactor cost

How Much Does a Commercial Trash Compactor Cost? Purchase, Installation, Rental, and the Hauler Math

2026-11-16 11 min read Norton Equipment

Published by Norton Equipment Co., serving commercial waste and recycling equipment customers from Byhalia, Mississippi since 1997.

The short answer

A new commercial trash compactor typically costs between about $8,000 for a small vertical unit and $60,000 or more for a large pre-crusher or auger, before installation. Self-contained compactors for wet waste commonly run $20,000 to $45,000 and stationary compactors $15,000 to $35,000 plus a receiver container. Installation adds a concrete pad, three-phase power, and often a chute or enclosure. Reconditioned machines cut the equipment price by roughly 30 to 60 percent.

The short version, then the long one

Search for the price of a commercial trash compactor and you get residential kitchen units, municipal fee schedules, and manufacturers who will not publish a number. This guide gives you the numbers a dealer actually works with: what each type of machine costs, what the installation adds, how a reconditioned unit changes the math, what the hauler’s “free” compactor really costs, and how to work out whether any of it pays back for your site. The ranges are planning ranges. The quote for your machine depends on specification, condition, site preparation, delivery, and installation, and that is the number to make decisions on.

What moves a compactor quote up or down

Two stationary compactors with the same charge box can differ by ten thousand dollars. These are the specifications behind the gap.

  • Charge box and container size. A larger charge box accepts bigger items and more volume per cycle. A larger receiver container or self-contained body means fewer pulls but a bigger machine and a bigger pad.
  • Compaction force and cycle time. Measured in pounds of ram force and seconds per cycle. More force packs denser loads and costs more in cylinder, power unit, and structure. High-force machines also demand more of the receiver container.
  • Power unit. Most commercial compactors run three-phase power at 208, 230, or 460 volts. A tri-voltage power unit, a larger motor for faster cycles, or a remote power unit mounted inside the building all add cost.
  • Liquid-tight construction. Self-contained units are fully welded with a sump to hold liquids. That construction is why they cost more than a stationary machine of similar capacity, and why they are the only right answer for wet waste.
  • Feed configuration. Ground feed is the base. Dock feed, through-wall chutes, doghouses, hoppers, and cart tippers are added steel, and are usually quoted as installation rather than equipment.
  • Controls and safety options. Key switches, remote start stations, full-container indicators, photo-eye auto start on chute-fed machines, and interlocked gates.
  • Steel options. Walk-on and drive-on decks, enclosures, and guards are fabricated per site. They are not optional when a safety audit or a landlord says they are not.

Installation costs, line by line

Installation is where first-time buyers get surprised, because the equipment quote is the number they remember and the site work is the number they pay. The compactor installation guide explains each item in detail. Here is what the line items are and what tends to drive each one. Ranges are intentionally wide; the site decides.

A useful rule: get every one of those lines in the quote before you sign anything, and ask which are included and which are allowances. A compactor quote that stops at the machine is not a quote for a working compactor.

How much a compactor saves per month

Hauling is billed per pull, not per pound. A compactor cuts the number of pulls, which is where the money is. The hauling bill article walks through the mechanism. The arithmetic you can do yourself looks like this.

  1. Count your pulls. How many times a week does a truck lift or pull a container, across every container on the site? Multiply by your per-pull charge, with surcharges, to get a monthly figure.
  2. Apply the compaction ratio. Dry commercial waste compacts at roughly 4 to 1. Divide weekly loose yards by four to get compacted yards, then divide by the receiver container size to see how often it fills.
  3. Price the new schedule. A roll-off pull costs more than a dumpster lift but happens far less often. Add container rent and disposal tonnage, which does not change much because the weight is the same.
  4. Subtract. The difference is the monthly saving. Divide the installed cost by it for the payback period.

A hypothetical example, the same one used elsewhere on this site so the numbers line up: a distribution operation running two 8-yard dumpsters pulled five times a week each at about $95 a pull with surcharges pays roughly $4,100 a month. The same volume through a 30-yard stationary compactor compacts into about one pull a week at roughly $450 plus disposal, about $2,300 a month with container rent. That is about $1,800 a month, or $21,600 a year. Against a $28,000 installed new machine the payback is around 16 months. Against a reconditioned machine it is under a year. Your invoices will produce a different number, which is why a waste stream consultation starts by reading them.

Rental and leasing

Norton does not advertise compactor rental, and most dealers who do are quoting a lease by another name. Leasing makes sense in three situations: a site you may leave within a few years, a capital budget that is closed for the year while the operating budget is open, and a seasonal operation that needs the machine for part of the year. In each case compare the total of lease payments against the owned cost above, ask who is responsible for maintenance and repairs, and read the end-of-term terms. A reconditioned machine often does what the lease was meant to do, at a lower total cost, with the machine yours at the end.

Questions to ask before you take a compactor quote

  • Is this the equipment price or the installed price, and which installation lines are included?
  • Will you quote new and reconditioned side by side?
  • Who services the machine after the install, and where do they dispatch from?
  • Does this deal tie my hauling contract to the equipment?
  • What warranty applies, and what does it exclude?
  • Will you model the payback from my own hauling invoices before I buy?

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a 30-yard self-contained compactor cost?
A new self-contained compactor in the 25 to 35 yard range typically falls toward the upper part of the $20,000 to $45,000 band before installation, depending on charge chamber size, compaction force, and liquid-handling options. Reconditioned units run 30 to 60 percent less. Installation is quoted separately.
What does it cost to install a commercial trash compactor?
Installation covers the concrete pad, a three-phase circuit and disconnect, any chute or through-wall door, delivery and rigging, commissioning, and permits. On a site with an existing pad and nearby three-phase power it can be modest. On a site that needs a new pad, a long power run, and a fabricated chute it can approach the cost of a smaller machine. Get every line quoted.
Is a used or reconditioned compactor worth it?
Usually, if the reconditioning was real. A compactor is a steel box with hydraulics, and a properly rebuilt machine with resealed hydraulics, serviced cylinders, repaired wear surfaces, and tested controls can run for another decade at 30 to 60 percent below new. Ask what was done, what is warrantied, and who services it afterward.
How much does a compactor save per month?
It depends on how many pulls you pay for now. Dry waste compacts about 4 to 1, so a site paying for daily dumpster lifts typically drops to one roll-off pull every week or two. Savings of several hundred to a couple of thousand dollars a month are common for mid-size commercial sites. Work it from your own invoices rather than a national average.
Who pays for the concrete pad?
The property owner or tenant, under whatever the lease says. The pad is site work, not equipment, so it is rarely included in a compactor price and never included in a hauler’s bundled equipment deal. Check whether your site already has a usable pad before assuming a new one.
Can I keep my current hauler with a compactor?
Yes. Stationary compactors use standard roll-off receiver containers and self-contained units are built to standard roll-off hoist specifications, so any hauler with a roll-off truck can service them. Owning the compactor yourself is what keeps that choice open at every contract renewal.
How long does a commercial compactor last?
With scheduled maintenance, 10 to 20 years is a reasonable expectation, and well-built machines go longer with a rebuild of hydraulics and wear surfaces along the way. Duty cycle, waste type, and whether leaks and faults are addressed early matter more than the calendar.
What voltage does a commercial compactor need?
Most run three-phase power at 208, 230, or 460 volts; many power units are tri-voltage and configured at installation. Some small vertical units run single-phase. Confirm the exact power unit specification before any electrical work is quoted, and check whether three-phase service is available at the dock.
