How Much Does a Cardboard Baler Cost? Vertical, Horizontal, Used, and Refurbished, With What a Bale Pays Back

2026-11-16 11 min read Norton Equipment

Published by Norton Equipment Co., serving commercial waste and recycling equipment customers from Byhalia, Mississippi since 1997.

The short answer

A new vertical cardboard baler typically costs $6,000 to $20,000 depending on chamber size and bale weight, with 60 inch machines in the middle of that range and 72 inch high-density models at the top. Horizontal balers start around $30,000 for a closed-door machine and climb with automation. Refurbished balers from Norton’s shop cost roughly half of new and carry a shop warranty. Used balers sold as-is cost less again. Installation for a vertical baler is usually modest.

Why nobody publishes baler prices, and why we do

Manufacturers sell through dealers, dealers quote installed, and every quote carries freight, rigging, and site conditions that vary. So the price pages you find are either residential trash compactors or a form that promises a quote. This guide gives you the ranges a dealer actually works from, by machine class and by condition, plus the costs around the machine and a worked example of what a bale pays back. The ranges are planning ranges. Your quote depends on the exact specification, condition, site preparation, delivery, and installation.

Norton has sold new, refurbished, and used balers across the Mid-South since 1997 and rebuilds them in our Byhalia shop. We quote all three side by side, which is the only honest way to answer the question in the title.

Two notes on the table. First, the 60 inch vertical is the most common commercial baler in America because it sits where most businesses’ volume does: a store or warehouse producing a few bales a week. Second, horizontal balers are ordered and built to the job, so their prices are quoted as a project that includes the feed system, power, and installation. The horizontal baler guide explains the three designs and what each one needs.

What changes the price of a baler

  • Chamber size and bale weight. The biggest factor. A wider, taller chamber means a heavier bale, a larger cylinder, a heavier frame, and a bigger motor.
  • Cylinder force and cycle time. High-density models use more hydraulic force to pack a heavier bale into the same chamber, which costs more in cylinder, pump, and structure, and is what makes a 72 inch bale mill-size.
  • Power. Single-phase 208 or 230 volt machines are the base for verticals. Three-phase and higher-horsepower power units add cost but cycle faster.
  • Doors, ejection, and controls. Interlocked doors are standard. Powered bale ejectors, bale chamber lights, full-bale indicators, and UL-listed control panels are options on some models and standard on others.
  • Safety package. Key-controlled switches, door interlocks, and the manufacturer’s ANSI Z245.5 compliance are what let the machine be used legally and, with the posted notice, loaded by 16 and 17 year olds. The baler safety and training guide covers the rules. Do not buy a machine without them.
  • Auto-tie and feed systems. On horizontals, the wire tier, conveyor, cart tipper, and controls are most of the price above the base machine.
  • Freight and rigging. A 60 inch vertical weighs on the order of two tons. Freight from the factory and placement in a back room are real costs, usually included in a dealer’s installed quote. Confirm.

Installation and site preparation for a baler

Here is the good news that compactor buyers do not get: a vertical baler is usually inexpensive to install. There is no concrete pad, no receiver container, and no roll-off truck approach. A typical vertical baler installation is a half day: deliver, set, anchor to the slab, connect to a dedicated circuit with a disconnect, test the interlocks and cycle, and train the crew. The items that add cost:

  • Power. A dedicated circuit and disconnect. Many verticals run single-phase; high-density and three-phase models need three-phase service at the location.
  • Floor. A level concrete floor rated for the machine and the bale. Most warehouse and back-room slabs qualify.
  • Clearance. Room for the door to swing, the bale to eject and tip, a pallet jack or forklift to take it away, and a technician to reach the power unit.
  • Access. The machine has to get through the building. Norton’s logistics crew sets verticals through retail back rooms routinely, after hours when needed.
  • Wire supply. A pallet of the right ties on day one.

Horizontal balers are a different project: a slab rated for 15,000 pounds and up, three-phase power sized to the power unit, a conveyor and possibly a pit, and crane or heavy forklift placement. Budget for installation the way you would for a compactor and get every line quoted.

Operating costs: wire, power, and maintenance

  • Baling wire. A vertical bale takes four or five single-loop or double-loop ties, so wire costs a few dollars per bale, and the right gauge and length matter more than the price per tie. Auto-tie horizontals use boxed or stem wire by the ton. The baling wire page covers matching wire to the machine; Norton delivers on scheduled routes.
  • Electricity. A vertical baler runs a 5 to 15 horsepower motor for under a minute per cycle. On a few bales a day the power cost is small enough to ignore. Horizontals running all shift are a line item, still modest against labor.
  • Maintenance. Hydraulic fluid and filter changes, hose inspection, door and latch adjustment, interlock and emergency stop testing. A quarterly preventive maintenance visit on a vertical is a few hundred dollars and catches the leaks and worn hoses that otherwise become a down machine. The baler maintenance checklist covers what operators watch between visits.
  • Labor. The real operating cost of a vertical is the operator’s time: a few minutes per cycle plus tying and ejection. When that time becomes a full shift, the horizontal conversation starts.

Does a baler pay for itself? A worked example

This example is hypothetical and illustrative. It is not a Norton customer result and your numbers will differ. The method is what matters.

A grocery store receives deliveries daily and fills an 8-yard dumpster with loose cardboard three times a week on top of its trash service, at about $95 a pull with surcharges. That is roughly $1,235 a month for cardboard pulls alone. The store installs a 60 inch vertical baler and produces about eight 700-pound bales a week, roughly 2.8 tons of OCC.

Against a new 60 inch baler installed at $12,000 the payback is eight to twelve months. Against a refurbished machine at roughly half that, it is well under a year. If the store sells bales at a mill-size price instead of a pickup rate, the return improves; if the recycler charges for pickup, it worsens. Three things decide your version of this table: how many pulls you pay for now, what a local buyer will do with your bales, and how many bales you will actually make. The hauling bill article covers the pull-count side and the bale weight guide covers what a bale weighs and is worth. Norton’s waste stream consultation runs the math from your own invoices at no charge.

On OCC pricing: published national averages for baled old corrugated containers moved through a wide range in 2026, roughly $60 to $100 a ton depending on the month and region. What a local recycler pays for a picked-up bale from a single store is lower than the published mill price and sometimes zero in exchange for free pickup. Ask for a written price and pickup schedule before you count on revenue.

Financing and leasing, in general terms

Most equipment lenders finance balers, and many dealers can introduce a finance partner. Typical structures are an equipment loan over three to five years or a fair-market-value lease with a purchase option at the end. Compare the total of payments against the cash price, confirm who carries maintenance, and watch the end-of-term terms on a lease. A refurbished machine often fits a cash budget that a new machine would push into financing, which is one of the reasons it is the most common choice for a first baler.

Questions to ask before you buy a baler

  • Is the price installed, with delivery, rigging, anchoring, power hookup, testing, and operator training included?
  • Does the machine meet ANSI Z245.5 and come with the documentation and the key-controlled switch?
  • What bale weight and dimensions will it produce with my material, and will my recycler accept them?
  • What wire does it take, and can you deliver it?
  • Will you quote new, refurbished, and used side by side?
  • Who services it, and from where?
  • Will you buy it back or trade it when I outgrow it?

Frequently Asked Questions

How much is a vertical cardboard baler?
New vertical balers typically run $6,000 to $20,000. Small 30 to 42 inch machines sit at the low end, 60 inch machines in the middle at roughly $9,000 to $15,000, and 72 inch high-density models at the top. Refurbished verticals cost roughly half of new, and used machines sold as-is cost less again. Installation for a vertical is usually a half day.
How much does a 72 inch baler cost?
A new 72 inch high-density vertical baler typically costs about $14,000 to $20,000, more with powered ejection, three-phase power units, or other options. It produces mill-size bales of roughly 750 to 1,100 pounds. A refurbished 72 inch machine from Norton’s shop costs roughly half of new.
Is a used baler worth it?
It can be, if you know what you are buying. A used baler is sold as-is: inspected and described, but not rebuilt and not warrantied. It suits buyers with their own maintenance crew, a need for a spare, or a strict budget. If you want the machine gone through and warrantied, a refurbished baler at roughly half of new is the lower-risk choice.
What does a refurbished baler cost?
Roughly half the cost of a comparable new machine. A Norton refurbishment resealed or rebuilt the cylinder, flushed and tested the hydraulics, inspected the chamber and platen, trued the doors, verified the interlocks, and cycle-tested the machine under load, and it ships with a shop warranty with written terms.
How much does baling wire cost per bale?
A vertical bale takes four or five single-loop or double-loop ties, so wire runs a few dollars per bale depending on gauge and length. Auto-tie horizontal balers use boxed or stem wire bought by weight. The right gauge for your machine and bale matters more than the price per tie, because under-gauge wire breaks and costs you the bale.
Can a baler pay for itself?
Often within a year or two. The saving comes from eliminating cardboard dumpster pulls and overflow charges, offset by wire and maintenance, plus whatever a recycler pays or saves you for picked-up bales. A store paying for several cardboard pulls a week commonly recovers a refurbished vertical in under a year. Run the math from your own invoices.
How long does a cardboard baler last?
A vertical baler is a thick steel chamber and a hydraulic cylinder. With hydraulic maintenance and interlock testing, 15 to 20 years of service is common, and a rebuild of the cylinder and hydraulics extends it further. Duty cycle and whether leaks are fixed early matter more than age.
Does Norton buy back balers?
Yes. Norton buys balers and compactors outright, takes trades when customers step up to a larger machine, and handles the rigging and removal. Many customers move from a refurbished 60 inch baler to a new 72 inch high-density machine with us a few years later.
