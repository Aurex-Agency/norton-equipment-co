Cardboard Bale Weight by Baler Size (and What a Bale Is Worth)

Updated 2026-11-16 7 min read Norton Equipment

Published by Norton Equipment Co., serving commercial waste and recycling equipment customers from Byhalia, Mississippi since 1997.

The short answer

A cardboard bale weighs anywhere from about 100 pounds out of a small vertical baler to 2,000 pounds or more out of a two-ram. A standard 60 inch vertical baler makes bales of roughly 600 to 1,000 pounds; a 72 inch high-density vertical makes mill-size bales of 750 to 1,100 pounds; auto-tie horizontals make 1,000 to 1,800 pound bales. Density, moisture, and the type of cardboard move the number.

Why bale weight matters

If you handle, transport, or sell cardboard, bale weight is the number everything else runs on. It decides which forklift moves the bale, how many bales fill a trailer, whether the recycler treats your material as mill-size, and what the bale is worth. It also decides which baler you should buy, because the chamber size sets the bale. This guide gives typical weights by machine, explains what moves them, answers the related question of how much the baler itself weighs, and puts a current value on the bale.

Cardboard bale weight by baler size

These are typical ranges for dry, well-compressed old corrugated containers (OCC). Your bales will land inside them if the machine is working to specification and the operator fills the chamber before tying.

What makes one bale heavier than another

  1. Chamber size. The biggest factor. A 72 inch chamber holds more cardboard than a 60 inch chamber at the same density.
  2. Compression force. High-density balers use more hydraulic force to pack more material into the same chamber. That is the difference between a standard 60 inch bale and a high-density one, and between a vertical bale and a horizontal one.
  3. How full the chamber is when the operator ties. Tying early makes light bales. Most verticals have a full-bale indicator; use it.
  4. Moisture. Wet cardboard weighs more and is worth less. Recyclers deduct for moisture and may reject soaked bales. Store cardboard and bales under cover.
  5. Type of cardboard. Heavy double-wall shipping cartons make denser bales than light single-wall retail packaging. Mixed paper and chipboard in the bale lower both weight and grade.
  6. Wire. Under-tied bales expand after ejection and lose density. Use the number and gauge of ties the machine manual calls for; the baling wire page covers the choices.

The machine, not the bale. This matters for the slab, the delivery truck, and the rigging plan.

What a bale of cardboard is worth in 2026

Recyclers and mills buy OCC by the ton, so a 1,000 pound bale is half a ton of commodity. Published national averages for baled OCC moved through a wide range during 2026, roughly between $60 and $100 a ton depending on the month and region, after several years of swings in both directions. Two cautions when you turn that into a number for your bales:

  • The published price is a mill price for truckload quantities of mill-size bales. A local recycler picking up a handful of bales from a single store pays less, sometimes nothing in exchange for free pickup. Ask for a written price and pickup schedule.
  • Density and cleanliness decide the grade. Dry, well-tied, cardboard-only bales from a high-density vertical or a horizontal qualify for the best treatment. Light, wet, or contaminated bales do not.

Even at a low pickup rate, the bale is doing its main job: cardboard that used to fill a dumpster you paid to have pulled is now leaving for free or for money. Handling bales safely

A 60 inch bale weighs more than most people expect and comes out of the chamber by tipping forward. Eject with the ram up and the machine stopped, stand clear of the tip path, and move bales only with a forklift rated for the weight and operated by a certified driver. Stack within the facility’s limit and keep bale storage out of aisles and away from exits. The baler safety and training guide covers ejection, handling, and the rest of operator training.

Choosing a baler around the bale

Start from the bale your recycler wants and your forklift can move, then work back to the machine. A store producing a few bales a week fits a 60 inch vertical. Serious volume or a recycler paying mill prices points to a 72 inch high-density vertical. When baling becomes a full shift, the horizontal conversation starts. Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a cardboard bale weigh?
Small bales from 30 inch vertical balers weigh about 100 to 300 pounds, 60 inch vertical bales 600 to 1,000 pounds, 72 inch high-density bales 750 to 1,100 pounds, auto-tie horizontal bales 1,000 to 1,800 pounds, and two-ram bales 1,200 to 2,000 pounds or more. Moisture, density, and cardboard type move the number within those ranges.
How much does a cardboard baler weigh?
A small 30 inch vertical baler weighs roughly 1,500 to 2,500 pounds, a 60 inch vertical 3,500 to 5,500 pounds, and a 72 inch high-density vertical 5,500 to 8,000 pounds. Horizontal balers run from about 10,000 to 40,000 pounds and two-ram machines from 40,000 pounds to well over 100,000. Check the manufacturer’s specification for the exact model.
What makes one bale heavier than another?
Chamber size, compression force, how full the chamber is when the operator ties, moisture, the type of cardboard, and proper tying. A dry, well-tied bale from a high-density baler weighs more per cubic foot and is worth more than a light or wet one.
How much is a bale of cardboard worth?
Published national averages for baled OCC ran roughly $60 to $100 a ton through 2026, so a 1,000 pound bale is worth about $30 to $50 at mill prices. A local recycler picking up a few bales from one store pays less, sometimes nothing in exchange for free pickup. The main saving is the dumpster pulls you no longer pay for.
